Case study · Telecom customer churn

Who leaves a telecom, and how early you can tell

I took a public dataset of 7,043 telecom customers and asked it the question I deal with in the field every month: which customers are about to go, what do they have in common, and what could a channel team do before they leave. Every number on this page comes from SQL I wrote and you can run yourself.

01 · THE QUESTION

What was I trying to find out?

Most churn projects stop at "build a model that predicts churn". That is not how a telecom business uses the answer. A field team needs three things: a short list of who to call this week, a reason those people are at risk, and a change at the point of sale that stops the next batch from becoming risky in the first place.

So I set four questions before writing any SQL:

  1. How big is the problem in customers and in revenue?
  2. When in the customer's life does churn happen?
  3. Which contract, product and payment patterns separate leavers from stayers?
  4. Can a handful of rules, simple enough for a supervisor to apply by hand, find most of the leavers?

The data and how I worked

The data is IBM's Telco Customer Churn sample, the version published on Kaggle: one row per customer of a fictional US telecom, with 21 columns covering contract, tenure in months, services, billing and whether the customer left in the last month.

I loaded the CSV into SQLite and built one clean view on top of it. TotalCharges arrives as text and is blank for 11 customers who had just joined, so I converted it and turned the blanks into nulls instead of dropping them. Churn became a 1 or 0, which makes AVG(churned) the churn rate directly, and tenure went into bands a sales team would actually use.

Show the cleaning view 
CREATE VIEW customers AS
SELECT
  customerID                                   AS customer_id,
  CAST(tenure AS INTEGER)                      AS tenure,
  CASE
    WHEN CAST(tenure AS INTEGER) <= 6  THEN '1. 0-6 months'
    WHEN CAST(tenure AS INTEGER) <= 12 THEN '2. 7-12 months'
    WHEN CAST(tenure AS INTEGER) <= 24 THEN '3. 13-24 months'
    WHEN CAST(tenure AS INTEGER) <= 48 THEN '4. 25-48 months'
    ELSE                                    '5. 49+ months'
  END                                          AS tenure_band,
  Contract                                     AS contract,
  InternetService                              AS internet,
  OnlineSecurity                               AS online_security,
  TechSupport                                  AS tech_support,
  PaymentMethod                                AS payment_method,
  CAST(MonthlyCharges AS REAL)                 AS monthly_charges,
  CAST(NULLIF(TRIM(TotalCharges), '') AS REAL) AS total_charges,
  CASE WHEN Churn = 'Yes' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END    AS churned
  -- plus the remaining service and demographic columns
FROM raw;
02 · WHAT SQL SHOWED

Six things the data says

In every chart the dashed line is the overall churn rate of 26.5%. Amber bars are the groups well above it.

1. Contract type decides almost everything

Month-to-month customers left at 42.7%. Customers on a two-year contract left at 2.8%, fifteen times less. Month-to-month customers are 55% of the base but 88.6% of all churn.

Show the SQL 
SELECT
  contract,
  COUNT(*)                        AS customers,
  SUM(churned)                    AS churned,
  ROUND(100.0 * AVG(churned), 1)  AS churn_pct,
  ROUND(100.0 * SUM(churned)
        / (SELECT SUM(churned) FROM customers), 1) AS share_of_all_churn
FROM customers
GROUP BY contract
ORDER BY churn_pct DESC;

2. The first months are where customers are lost

More than half of customers in their first six months had already left (52.9%). In month one alone it was 62%. Using a running total, 42% of all churners left within six months and 55.5% within a year. After four years churn falls to 9.5%.

Month-to-month stays risky for longer, though: even after four years those customers churn at 26%, while long-tenure contract customers barely move.

Show the SQL (running share with a window function) 
WITH by_month AS (
  SELECT tenure, COUNT(*) AS customers, SUM(churned) AS churned
  FROM customers
  WHERE tenure BETWEEN 1 AND 12
  GROUP BY tenure
)
SELECT
  tenure,
  customers,
  churned,
  ROUND(100.0 * churned / customers, 1) AS churn_pct,
  ROUND(100.0 * SUM(churned) OVER (ORDER BY tenure)
        / (SELECT SUM(churned) FROM customers), 1) AS cumulative_share_of_churn
FROM by_month
ORDER BY tenure;

3. Fiber customers without support are the weakest spot

Fiber had the highest churn of any internet type at 41.9%, and also the highest average bill at $91.50 a month. The difference comes from support. Fiber customers with neither online security nor tech support left at 55%; fiber customers with either one left at 24.5%. The same pattern holds on DSL, at 35.4% against 11%.

Show the SQL (counting add-ons in a CTE) 
WITH add_ons AS (
  SELECT
    churned,
    (online_security   = 'Yes') + (online_backup    = 'Yes') +
    (device_protection = 'Yes') + (tech_support     = 'Yes') +
    (streaming_tv      = 'Yes') + (streaming_movies = 'Yes') AS n_add_ons
  FROM customers
  WHERE internet <> 'No'
)
SELECT n_add_ons,
       COUNT(*)                       AS customers,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(churned), 1) AS churn_pct
FROM add_ons
GROUP BY n_add_ons
ORDER BY n_add_ons;

4. Customers who pay by hand leave more

Customers paying by electronic check churned at 45.3%, almost three times the rate of customers on automatic bank or card payments (15 to 17%). Electronic check users are a third of the base but 57% of everyone who left. Paperless billing customers also left twice as often, 33.6% against 16.3%.

Churn rate by payment method

Electronic check45.3%
Mailed check19.1%
Bank transfer, automatic16.7%
Credit card, automatic15.2%

Average, 26.5%

My reading: a payment the customer has to make every month is a monthly decision to stay or go. An automatic payment removes that decision.

5. Household matters, gender does not

Senior citizens churned at 41.7% against 23.6% for everyone else. Customers without a partner (33%) or without dependents (31.3%) also left more. Men and women churned at the same rate, 26.2% and 26.9%, so gender is not worth targeting on.

6. Price matters, but in the middle more than at the top

Churn rises with the bill from 10.9% under $35 a month to 38% between $70 and $90, then dips to 32.9% above $90. The highest payers usually hold many services, which keeps them. The customers paying a lot for a thin bundle are the ones who feel the price.

Put the findings together and one group stands out. These 764 customers are about a tenth of the base, yet nearly three in four of them left, and they alone explain close to 30% of all churn and $45,486 of lost monthly revenue.

A field team cannot work only one profile, so I also built a score anyone can calculate without a model. One point each for month-to-month, first 12 months, fiber, no security or support, and electronic check.

Show the SQL for the score 
WITH scored AS (
  SELECT churned,
    (contract = 'Month-to-month')
  + (tenure <= 12)
  + (internet = 'Fiber optic')
  + (internet <> 'No' AND online_security = 'No'
                      AND tech_support    = 'No')
  + (payment_method = 'Electronic check') AS risk_points
  FROM customers
)
SELECT risk_points,
       COUNT(*)                       AS customers,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(churned), 1) AS churn_pct,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(churned)
             / (SELECT SUM(churned) FROM customers), 1) AS share_of_all_churn
FROM scored
GROUP BY risk_points
ORDER BY risk_points;
04 · THE FIELD VIEW

What I would do about it as a channel manager

I lead a 104-person field team in the Chandpur Region, built 45 Robi Sales and Service Points from scratch, and helped take a home internet product to 1,500+ households. The SQL tells me who leaves. This part is what I would actually change on the ground.

  1. Treat the first 90 days as part of the sale

    Churn is highest in month one and more than half of it happens in the first year. A sale that is not looked after in those weeks is often a sale we lose. I would give every new customer a named contact at the nearest sales point, with a check in at day 7, day 30 and day 60: is it working, do they understand the bill, do they need help.

    From the data: 62% churn in month one, 42% of all churners gone within six months.

  2. Pay the field for customers who stay, not only for activations

    If incentives only count gross adds, the field has every reason to sell the easiest plan to whoever walks in and move on. I would count part of each person's incentive only when the customer is still active after 90 days. That one change pushes the team toward longer plans, bundles and proper setup without a single extra instruction.

    From the data: month-to-month is 55% of customers and 88.6% of churn.

  3. Sell the commitment and the support at the counter

    Customers on longer plans and customers with support or extra services leave far less. At the point of sale that means offering a longer validity plan with a real reason to take it, and making setup help and a support channel part of every home internet sale rather than an extra. For a product like home internet, installation and the first week of help are the support.

    From the data: two-year contracts 2.8% churn; fiber without support 55% against 24.5% with it.

  4. Make staying automatic

    Every manual payment is a monthly moment to reconsider. I would push auto renewal and wallet based auto pay at the time of sale, and give a small, clear reason to switch on it, such as a bonus on the first auto renewal.

    From the data: electronic check 45.3% churn against 15 to 17% for automatic payment.

  5. Turn the score into a weekly call list

    A risk score is only useful if someone acts on it on Monday morning. I already run a morning brief with an action list for my supervisors in the RSSP tracker I built. The churn score would sit in the same place: each supervisor gets the 4 and 5 point customers in their area, with the reason they are flagged, and logs the outcome of each call.

    From the data: the 4 to 5 point group is 21% of customers and 51% of churn.

  6. Prove it with a test before rolling it out

    I would run the first 90 day programme in half of the areas and keep the other half as they are, then compare how many new customers are still active after three months. If the difference is real, roll it out to every area. If not, the data saved us from spending the field's time on the wrong thing.

What changes in a market like Bangladesh

This dataset is a US postpaid business with contracts and monthly bills. Most mobile customers in Bangladesh are prepaid, so a customer rarely cancels. They simply stop recharging and the SIM goes quiet. That means churn has to be defined from behaviour, for example no recharge or usage for a set number of days, and the warning signs are falling recharge amounts and days since the last recharge, not a contract type.

The lessons still carry over. The first weeks matter most, a thin bundle is easy to leave, a customer who recharges by hand decides again every time, and the people best placed to keep a customer are the ones who sold to them.

05 · LIMITS AND NEXT STEPS

What this analysis can and can't say

  • It is a snapshot. Each row shows a customer at one point in time, so the tenure results compare different customers, not the same customers over time.
  • These are patterns, not causes. Electronic check users may differ in ways the data doesn't show. The way to know whether moving them to auto pay helps is a test, like the one above.
  • The company is fictional. The patterns are realistic for a US postpaid telecom, but the numbers should not be read as any real operator's figures.
  • The score is rules, not a model. That is deliberate, so a supervisor can check it by hand. My next step is a logistic regression in Python to see how much better a model ranks customers than these five rules, and whether that gain is worth the loss in simplicity.
